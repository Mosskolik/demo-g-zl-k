## Q1. What did the Beal et al. "Working memory and math problem solving by blind middle and high school students" project find?

### Takeaway
[BLIND] When math word problems were read aloud by text-to-speech, blind middle schoolers did about as well as sighted students on the same tutoring system. Accuracy was 82% on arithmetic problems and 59% on fractions problems, and students listened to each problem about twice. The weak point was the math (unlike-denominator fractions), not the length of the problem or the audio. I could not confirm the "37–87 words" range or the final length and readability effects.

### Cited Findings
- [BLIND] Project aim: "investigate how blind students solve math word problems varying in text length and grade-level readability, in the context of an existing web-based tutorial." It tracked replay actions as a proxy for working-memory load, so that the software could choose problems suitable for blind students. — [Paths to Literacy summary](https://www.pathstoliteracy.org/research/working-memory-and-math-problem-solving-blind-middle-and-high-school-students-implications); [Paths to Literacy resource page](https://www.pathstoliteracy.org/resource/working-memory-and-math-problem-solving-blind-middle-and-high-school-students-implications/)
- [BLIND] Beal and Shaw (2008) varied text length and grade-level readability inside the existing web tutor (AnimalWatch-type system). — [Beal 2009 PDF, Univ. of Arizona](https://smsf.cs.arizona.edu/projects/focal/ergalics/files/beal_1209_1.pdf)
- [BLIND] JOLT 2009 paper: the existing system was adapted so that text-to-speech presented the word problems as audio and also gave audio feedback on answers. In the evaluation with blind middle-school students (N = 11), their problem solving "was comparable to that of sighted students who had worked with the original system." — [Beal et al., MERLOT JOLT vol. 5 no. 4 (2009)](https://jolt.merlot.org/vol5no4/beal_1209.htm)
- [BLIND] Accuracy was 82% correct on arithmetic word problems and 59% on fractions word problems. Students listened about twice per problem, and the count was similar for both problem types. — [JOLT](https://jolt.merlot.org/vol5no4/beal_1209.htm) (as extracted by search; full text not fetched); [academia.edu copy](https://www.academia.edu/67573445/Working_memory_and_math_problem_solving_by_blind_middle_and_high_school_students_Implications_for_universal_access)
- [BLIND] Performance dropped on problems that needed a common denominator for unlike fractions, "regardless of the problem length." — [JOLT](https://jolt.merlot.org/vol5no4/beal_1209.htm) (via search extract)
- [GENERAL, sighted middle school] A separate study of middle-school tutoring problems found that story-text length mattered for readability even after mathematical difficulty was controlled. — [Learning Disabilities: A Contemporary Journal 21(2), 2023, ERIC](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1399914.pdf)
- [GENERAL, less-skilled readers] A 2025 EDM paper reported that shorter texts were, unexpectedly, linked to problems that were harder for less-skilled readers. In other words, shorter does not automatically mean easier, because compressed text can be denser. — [EDM 2025 short paper](https://educationaldatamining.org/EDM2025/proceedings/2025.EDM.short-papers.189/index.html)

### Inferences
- Within the lengths tested, blind students listened to each item about twice, whatever the problem type. That points to a design default of **one replay per item as normal behaviour**. A replay button should be free, prominent, and should not count as a penalty.
- The gap between arithmetic and fractions seems to come from the conceptual difficulty of the math, not from audio overload. Simplifying the language will not fix a conceptual barrier, so scaffold the math step (for example, "first find a common denominator") separately from the wording.
- Since length did not change the fractions drop, moderate word-problem lengths seem manageable for blind students in audio. But this rests on one small sample (N = 11).

### Gaps
- **Not verified:** the "37–87 words" range and any readability statistics (for example Flesch-Kincaid grade levels). The full text could not be fetched, and no search result quoted those numbers.
- **Not verified:** any statistical relationship between word count or readability and replay count or accuracy in the final results. A search summary said directly that these results were not found in what was available.
- The high-school data mentioned in the title was not found.

## Q2. What do cognitive load theory and auditory working-memory research say about audio-only math?

### Takeaway
[GENERAL] Speech is transient: once heard, it is gone. Long or complex spoken passages therefore overload working memory and can cancel out the usual benefit of audio (the "transient information effect"). The best-supported remedies are short, simple chunks; segmenting with pauses the learner controls; signaling or cueing; and self-pacing. Almost all of this evidence comes from sighted learners using audio plus visuals. [BLIND] Studies of spoken math with blind or visually impaired users back up the same picture: recall falls steeply once an expression has more than a certain number of elements, especially in audio-only form, and pauses inside expressions help listeners tell structures apart.

### Cited Findings
- [GENERAL] Leahy and Sweller (2011, *Applied Cognitive Psychology*): the modality effect can disappear or reverse when complex information is spoken. They concluded that long, complex passages should not be given in auditory form. — [Macquarie University research record](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/cognitive-load-theory-and-the-effects-of-transient-information-on/)
- [GENERAL] Leahy and Sweller (2016, *Instructional Science*): longer audio text was worse than equivalent written text. Even "shorter" audio still underperformed until it was shortened further, and only then did audio-plus-visual beat visual alone. Their advice: give technical verbal information as audio only in "small, simple chunks." — [Springer DOI 10.1007/s11251-015-9362-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-015-9362-9)
- [GENERAL] Long, complex spoken texts paired with a diagram can harm learning unless segmentation and learner-paced instruction reduce the transient information effect. — [KoreaScience 2016](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201612460477292.page)
- [GENERAL] Sweller's definition: the transient information effect is when learners given information in permanent form score higher than learners given the same information in transient form. — [Cambridge Core chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/670D96C3B9520320CE558AA855A49EE9)
- [GENERAL] Segmenting meta-analysis (Rey et al., 2019; 56 investigations): significant small-to-medium effects on retention and transfer. Segmenting lowered rated cognitive load, but that load effect was not reliable when learners already set their own pace. — [Rey et al. 2019 PDF](https://maria-wirzberger.de/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Rey2019_Article_AMeta-analysisOfTheSegmentingE.pdf); [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330143605_A_Meta-analysis_of_the_Segmenting_Effect)
- [GENERAL] Signaling meta-analysis (Schneider et al., 2018; 103 studies, 12,201 participants): moderate effects on retention (g+ = .53) and transfer (g+ = .33). Sources disagree on whether it reduces cognitive load. One analysis reports a non-significant load effect (g = .25, 95% CI [-.04, .55]). — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321270303_A_meta-analysis_of_how_signaling_affects_learning_with_media)
- [GENERAL] A cueing meta-analysis and meta-regression: cues help learning because they reduce total cognitive load and prevent overload. — [PMC5576760](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5576760/)
- [GENERAL] Noetel et al. (2022) meta-meta-analysis of multimedia design: replacing on-screen text with audio narration (the modality principle, in visual-plus-audio settings) improved transfer (d = 0.76) and reduced cognitive load (d = 0.62). Self-pacing, segmenting and personalized (conversational) language also improved learning. — [Noetel et al. 2022, Review of Educational Research (SAGE)](https://journals.sagepub.com/stoken/default+domain/PU8EQQCTDRBZ2SADDIRW/full)
- [BLIND] Riga and Kouroupetroglou, "How Blind Individuals Recall Mathematical Expressions in Auditory, Tactile, and Auditory–Tactile Modalities" (16 blind participants). Recall shows a threshold: past a certain number of elements it drops fast, and errors rise with complexity much more steeply in the auditory-only condition. Deletions were the most common error, and operators were the hardest elements to remember. — [MDPI Multimodal Technologies and Interaction 8(7):57](https://mdpi.com/2414-4088/8/7/57); [DOAJ record](https://libmast.utm.my/Record/doaj.art-19852e3401f64238b0332405f1f19e76)
- [BLIND/mixed] EAR-Math (Kacorri, Riga, Kouroupetroglou 2014): a pilot evaluation of audio-rendered math (MathPlayer, Greek) with 7 participants and 39 stimuli. — [EAR-Math PDF](https://terpconnect.umd.edu/~hernisa/pub/2014-uahci-kacorri-riga-kouroupetroglou.pdf); [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-07440-5_11)
- [GENERAL/mixed] Inserting pauses inside spoken math expressions greatly improved listeners' ability to tell apart two similar algebraic expressions. — [Evaluating prosodic cues to disambiguate algebraic expressions, CWU Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.cwu.edu/compsci/22)
- [Mixed, VI-focused] Ferreira and Freitas (2005, AudioMath) used short and long pauses to signal hierarchy in an expression. They concluded that prosody in math needs more study and that **navigation functionality is necessary**. This is reported in the ETS report by Frankel and Brownstein (2016, RR-16-33), which evaluated the prosodic and lexical cues of the ClearSpeak style for students with visual impairments. — [ETS RR-16-33 PDF (ERIC)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1124730.pdf); [ETS report page](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2016/jwvb.html); [Wiley DOI 10.1002/ets2.12119](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ets2.12119)
- [Mixed, VI-focused] ClearSpeak speech style (ETS RR-16-23): besides wording, speech styles can specify pausing and other prosodic cues inside an expression. — [ETS ClearSpeak report page](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2016/jwog.html)
- [BLIND] Process-Driven Math (PDM) uses chunking to "simplify the landscape" of complex algebraic expressions and reduce working-memory load. Numbers, variables and operators are hidden and revealed in layers, as the student asks for them. — [Gulley, Auburn dissertation 2021](https://etd.auburn.edu/handle/10415/7656); [NWRESD BVI math resources: PDM](https://sites.google.com/nwresd.k12.or.us/bvimathresources/tools/process-driven-math)

### Inferences
- For an audio-only math app, the transient information effect is the main design constraint. Every spoken unit should be small enough to hold in mind until the next one arrives, and the learner should be able to hear it again (permanence by replay).
- Segmenting plus learner control (a "next" button, or a pause after each chunk) is the most transferable principle. Since segmenting adds little when learners already self-pace, a learner-controlled "next" in effect *is* segmenting.
- Signaling in audio means verbal markers ("First...", "Second fact...", "The question is...") and prosodic cues (pauses at structural boundaries). Pauses inside expressions have direct evidence for helping disambiguation.
- Operators were the most-forgotten elements in recall by blind users, so say operators clearly and consider repeating them. Prefer lexical bracketing ("the quantity x plus 1, end quantity" / ClearSpeak-style) to bare "open/close parenthesis."
- The redundancy principle (do not duplicate the same words in two channels) mostly concerns on-screen text plus narration. In audio-only it suggests not wrapping core facts in filler words, but deliberate *repetition on request* (replay) is different from forced redundancy.

### Gaps
- I found no study that directly tests segmenting, signaling or pacing for **audio-only** math word problems with **blind** students. The meta-analyses are almost all visual-plus-narration with sighted learners.
- I could not get the exact element-count threshold from Riga and Kouroupetroglou (full text not fetched).
- I could not get the specific ETS RR-16-33 results (which prosodic or lexical cues helped).
- I found no evidence-based optimal pause duration in seconds.

## Q3. How do math accessibility tools let users replay or navigate parts of expressions, and is there evidence that this helps?

### Takeaway
[BLIND, tools] Current tools (MathCAT, MathJax explorer, Access8Math, StereoMath, PDM) all give hierarchical navigation: zoom in and out by subexpression, step through pieces, "where am I," go back, adjustable verbosity, and on-demand summaries. Research groups (ETS/AudioMath) conclude that navigation is *necessary*, and design papers argue it reduces working-memory load. But I found no controlled study showing that navigation or replay improves comprehension over linear speech for blind students.

### Cited Findings
- [Tool] MathCAT navigation: move by subexpression ("Enhanced," the default), "Simple" piece, or character. Shift+Up/Down switches mode. Up/Down zoom out and in. Enter gives "Where am I." Backspace returns to the last position. — [MathCAT Navigation Commands](https://nsoiffer.github.io/MathCAT/nav-commands.html)
- [Tool] MathCAT settings: "speech after move" chooses whether the expression is *read* or *described* (summary or outline) after each move. NavVerbosity is Terse, Medium or Full. AutoZoomOut of 2D expressions is on by default. — [MathCAT user docs (docs.rs)](https://docs.rs/crate/mathcat/0.1.26/source/docs/users.md); [MathCAT GitHub users.md](https://github.com/NSoiffer/MathCAT/blob/e29f6a9dd49c5472d3b34f9084e3d741a7e106cf/docs/users.md); [MathCAT home (DAISY)](https://daisy.github.io/MathCAT)
- [Tool] MathJax has an equivalent keyboard "explorer" for walking through expression trees. — [MathJax Explorer Keyboard Commands](https://docs.mathjax.org/en/latest/basic/explorer-commands.html)
- [Tool] Access8Math (NVDA add-on) also offers math reading and navigation. — [Access8Math GitHub](https://github.com/tsengwoody/Access8Math)
- [BLIND, design] StereoMath (Ge and Seo, ASSETS '24): an equation editor that uses spatial (left-right) audio matching each element's rendered position. A keyboard "spatial nav mode" treats the keyboard as a map of the layout, and the equation is modeled as both a tree and a grid. Earcons mark the end of text, entry into an editable field, and arrival at nodes. Linear and equation navigation styles can be switched, verbosity adjusted, and spatial audio turned off. It cites prior work that blind users spend more than 30% longer on navigation than sighted users in complex interfaces, and it frames its research questions around depth of understanding and cognitive load. — [arXiv 2501.01404v2 (HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.01404v2); [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.01404v2); [Illinois Experts record](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/stereomath-an-accessible-and-musical-equation-editor/)
- [BLIND, method] PDM: the student directs when numbers, variables and operators are revealed, in chunks and layers, to get the overall structure first without overloading working memory. Gulley's dissertation qualitatively studied 25 students at three state schools for the blind. Its experimental part (more than 650 students with and without disabilities) found that students who reported difficulty concentrating scored higher with PDM. Students wanted optional visual and tactile elements as well. NWEA built PDM into a Microsoft-funded prototype for accessible math assessment. — [Gulley 2021, Auburn ETD](https://etd.auburn.edu/handle/10415/7656); [full record](https://etd.auburn.edu/handle/10415/7656?show=full); [NWEA, "Looking beyond vision" PDF](https://www.nwea.org/uploads/2020/03/Looking-beyond-vision-Supports-for-students-who-are-blind-or-visually-impaired-in-mathematics.pdf); [NWRESD PDM page](https://sites.google.com/nwresd.k12.or.us/bvimathresources/tools/process-driven-math)
- [Mixed, VI-focused] Ferreira and Freitas (AudioMath) concluded that navigation functionality is necessary. ETS's work on synthesized speech of math pairs human-like pausing with interactive navigation as the two main levers. — [ETS RR-16-33 (ERIC)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1124730.pdf); [AudioMath project (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221009916_Enhancing_the_Accessibility_of_Mathematics_for_Blind_People_The_AudioMath_Project)
- [BLIND, behavior] In Beal's audio tutor, students used replay about twice per problem. — [JOLT](https://jolt.merlot.org/vol5no4/beal_1209.htm) (via search extract)

### Inferences
- Useful features for a spoken word-problem app, mapped from these tools:
  - **Replay whole problem**
  - **Replay last sentence or chunk** (the Backspace equivalent)
  - **Next and previous chunk** (subexpression or sentence navigation)
  - **"What is the question?"** jump (the "where am I" equivalent)
  - **Summary or overview first, then details** (MathCAT's "describe" mode, PDM's structure-first layering)
  - **Verbosity levels** (terse for experts, full for beginners)
  - **Adjustable speech rate**
- "Overview then detail" is a recurring design pattern across MathCAT, PDM and StereoMath. It fits schema-building in cognitive load terms.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled or quantitative evidence (in what I could reach) that navigation or replay features *improve comprehension or accuracy* compared with linear playback for blind students. StereoMath's user-study results were not in the search extracts.
- I found no published documentation of ChromeVox or JAWS math navigation in the results.

## Q4. Do blind students have stronger verbal working memory, and what does that imply?

### Takeaway
[BLIND] Yes, fairly consistently. Congenitally blind adults and children (from about 6–10 years old) outperform sighted peers on *verbal* span tasks (forward and backward letter or digit span, listening span), but not on spatial tasks. Some blind listeners also follow synthetic speech at about 2–3 times the rate sighted listeners can. The studies are small (about 12–20 per group) and I found no meta-analysis. Implication: do not "dumb down" content or slow speech by default. But for spatial or structural math content, and for long transient passages, the advantage does not remove the need for chunking.

### Cited Findings
- [BLIND] Blind adults outperformed sighted controls on forward and backward letter span and on a complex letter span task. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2020, 10.3389/fpsyg.2020.01571](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.01571/pdf); ["Perks of blindness: Enhanced verbal memory span in blind over sighted adults" (OUCI record)](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4MnBD1xl)
- [BLIND] Congenitally blind participants were significantly better than sighted controls on all verbal tasks, with no significant difference on spatial tasks. A blind advantage on backward digit span has also been reported (Occelli et al., 2017). — [PMC5436932](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5436932); [Edge Hill University, "Enhanced verbal abilities in the congenitally blind"](https://research.edgehill.ac.uk/en/publications/enhanced-verbal-abilities-in-the-congenitally-blind/)
- [BLIND, children] 10-year-old blind children outperformed sighted children on listening word span and backward digit span. Memory advantages have been documented from about age 6. — [PMC7347789](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7347789); [PMC 9204649](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/9204649)
- [BLIND] The advantage is larger for verbal than for nonverbal recognition in the same blind participants (2021). — [Experimental Brain Research 2021, s00221-021-06304-4](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00221-021-06304-4.pdf)
- [BLIND] Proposed mechanisms: memory practice driven by blindness (blind people rely more on working memory to process information) and recruitment of "visual" cortex for verbal processing. — [2022 Exp. Brain Res. paper PDF](https://noraharhen.github.io/papers/2022_ahlb_ebr.pdf); [PMC 9204649](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/9204649)
- [BLIND] Some blind people understand ultra-fast speech up to about 22 syllables per second, versus about 8 syl/s for sighted listeners. This involves the primary visual cortex and builds up through repeated exposure to accelerated speech. — [Dietrich et al., PMC3847124 (BMC Neuroscience 2013)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3847124); [ASA lay summary](https://acoustics.org/pressroom/httpdocs/161st/Dietrich.html); [Moos & Trouvain, ICPhS 2007](https://icphs2007.de/conference/Papers/1186/index.html)
- [BLIND/LV] In a large crowdsourced study (Bragg et al., CHI 2018), blind and low-vision listeners had higher listening rates, attributed to early exposure to fast synthetic speech. — [Bragg CHI 2018 PDF](https://wildlab.cs.washington.edu/Publications_files/Bragg_CHI2018.pdf); [Expanding a Large Inclusive Study PDF](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~dkbragg/papers/Expanding_a_Large_Inclusive_Study.pdf)
- [BLIND] Even so, audio-only recall of math expressions by blind participants collapses past an element threshold and is worse than tactile or combined presentation. Enhanced verbal span does not remove the capacity limit for math structure. — [Riga & Kouroupetroglou, MDPI MTI 8(7):57](https://mdpi.com/2414-4088/8/7/57)

### Inferences
- Do not over-simplify vocabulary or slow down speech for blind users as a default. Offer speech-rate control (experienced screen-reader users may want much faster than default TTS) and terse versus verbose modes.
- The advantage is verbal-sequential, not spatial. Math with spatial or 2D structure (fractions, nested parentheses, tables, geometry) still needs explicit structural signaling and chunking. This fits Beal's finding that fractions were the weak point.
- Blind students' verbal advantage is likely *trained*, so it varies a lot between individuals: congenital versus late blindness, braille and screen-reader experience. Adjustable settings beat a single fixed design.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis or pooled effect size. The studies are small.
- I found no study that links blind students' verbal working-memory span directly to spoken *math word-problem* performance.
- Data on late-blind and low-vision students is thin in what I found.

## Q5. Recommended sentence length, facts per sentence, and ordering (question first or last) for spoken word problems

### Takeaway
I found no study that gives an evidence-based sentence length or "facts per sentence" figure specifically for spoken problems for blind students. The closest evidence is [GENERAL]: (a) linguistic simplification (shorter sentences, familiar words, active voice, no irrelevant information) raises math word-problem scores without changing the math construct, especially for weaker readers; (b) **putting the question before the problem text improves performance**, more so for weaker students and harder problems; (c) audio should come in "small, simple chunks."

### Cited Findings
- [GENERAL, 4th graders] Thevenot, Devidal, Barrouillet and Fayol (2007, *QJEP* 60(1):43–56) found the classic question-position effect: performance is better when the question comes before the text. The effect was stronger for children with weaker math skills and for harder problems, and the authors explain it with a situation-model account. — [Univ. Geneva open archive](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:88300); [Serval UNIL record](https://serval.unil.ch/notice/serval:BIB_09D260C9B51E); [Taylor & Francis / informahealthcare](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/17470210600587927)
- [GENERAL, Grade 8 / ELL] Abedi and Lord (2001), "The language factor in mathematics tests": they rewrote 69 NAEP math items to simplify non-math vocabulary and sentence structure while leaving the math terms alone, and math experts confirmed the versions were parallel. Most students' scores rose, especially English learners'. — [Abedi & Lord 2001 PDF (typeset.io)](https://typeset.io/pdf/the-language-factor-in-mathematics-tests-1o16uxplej.pdf)
- [GENERAL] Shorter sentences reduce complexity because they are less morphologically and syntactically complex (Abedi, Lord & Plummer, 1995). Unfamiliar or rare words and passive voice hinder comprehension, and adding irrelevant information lowers success even when the math is unchanged. — [De Gruyter chapter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9783110661941-017/pdf); [Springer, Cognitive Processing 10.1007/s10339-019-00948-5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10339-019-00948-5)
- [GENERAL] An IES/NCEE evaluation: math items can be linguistically modified to reduce language complexity without altering the construct being measured. — [IES/NCEE report](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/resource-library/report/evaluation-report/accommodations-english-language-learner-students-effect-linguistic-modification-math-test-item-sets)
- [GENERAL] Audio technical information should be in "small, simple chunks." Long, complex passages should not be spoken. — [Leahy & Sweller 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-015-9362-9)
- [GENERAL] Personalized (conversational) language improves learning in multimedia instruction. — [Noetel et al. 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/stoken/default+domain/PU8EQQCTDRBZ2SADDIRW/full)
- [BLIND] Within the problem lengths Beal tested, length did not explain the fractions drop, and students replayed about twice per problem. — [JOLT](https://jolt.merlot.org/vol5no4/beal_1209.htm) (via search extract)

### Inferences (practical guidance synthesized from the above; not directly tested with blind students)
- **Order:** state the goal or question first ("We want to find how many apples are left."), then give the facts, then **repeat the question at the end** just before the answer prompt. Question-first evidence comes from sighted 4th graders, but the logic is stronger in audio: knowing the goal tells the listener which facts to keep in working memory, and there is no page to scan back over. Repeating at the end makes up for the transience of speech.
- **One fact (one number or one relation) per sentence.** Short, active, declarative sentences, and avoid embedded clauses. This is an inference from Abedi and Lord's simplification features combined with Leahy and Sweller's "small, simple chunks." There is no evidence-based word count. As a hedged heuristic only: keep each spoken sentence short enough to hold one quantity and its referent.
- **Remove irrelevant information** (unless distractor discrimination is itself the learning goal), keep math vocabulary unchanged, and keep the math difficulty the same. Simplify language, not content (Abedi and Lord, IES), which fits blind students' strong verbal abilities.
- **Insert pauses** between facts and at structural boundaries in expressions, and use verbal signals ("first," "then," "the question is") (pause-disambiguation study, signaling meta-analysis).
- **Offer replay** of the whole problem, the last sentence, and "repeat the question," plus adjustable speech rate and verbosity (Beal replay behaviour, MathCAT, PDM).
- For harder or 2D content (fractions, nested expressions), give an overview first ("a fraction: the top is ..., the bottom is ...") and then let the learner step through pieces (PDM, MathCAT describe mode, Riga and Kouroupetroglou threshold).

### Gaps
- I found no empirical recommendation for words per sentence, facts per sentence, or total word count for *spoken* problems for blind students.
- I found no study testing question position in *audio* presentation or with blind learners.
- I could not verify the Beal "37–87 words" range, so it cannot serve as an evidence-based length guideline here.
