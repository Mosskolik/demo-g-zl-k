# Evidence-Based Methods for Teaching Mathematics to Blind and Low-Vision Secondary Students (implications for an audio-only math voice assistant)

> Method note (read first): In this session, full-text fetching was blocked for nfb.org, tandfonline.com and files.eric.ed.gov (network allowlist). All findings below come from search-engine abstracts, ERIC/publisher records and search snippets, not from reading the full papers. Exact numbers flagged "(snippet)" should be checked against the full text before they are quoted as precise figures in the final Turkish report. No effect sizes (d, g, Tau, PND) specific to VI math interventions could be verified; where none is given, none was found.

## 1. What do systematic reviews and single-case studies conclude about effective math instruction for students with visual impairments (VI)?

### Takeaway
The evidence base is thin and mostly small-N: reviews find only about 5–11 qualifying studies, mostly single-case or qualitative, and no verified pooled effect sizes. The consistent conclusions are: (a) students with VI and no cognitive disability can keep pace with grade-level math; (b) a combination of tools works best (braille/Nemeth, abacus, tactile and concrete materials); (c) explicit, systematic, sequenced instruction is central, especially for graphics; (d) audio/digital tools reach about the same accuracy as braille or print but have not been shown to be better.

### Cited Findings
**Systematic and narrative reviews**
- Brawand & Johnson (2016), *Journal of Blindness Innovation and Research* 6(1), DOI 10.5241/6-86. This is a literature review, not a meta-analysis. It concludes that "a combination of the abacus, Braille codes, tactile materials, and concrete materials" best meets the needs of students with VI. It notes that math "is highly visual in nature and often uses graphics to convey important information, presenting an additional obstacle." — [NFB JBIR abstract](https://nfb.org/images/nfb/publications/jbir/jbir16/jbir060101abs.html); [full text URL (not fetched)](https://nfb.org/images/nfb/publications/jbir/jbir16/jbir060101.html)
- Brawand & Johnson also recommend *programmed instruction*, meaning "a sequence of instructional activities," which "has the potential to maximize learning and increase motivation for students with visual impairments" (citing Agrawal, 2004). — [NFB JBIR](https://nfb.org/images/nfb/publications/jbir/jbir16/jbir060101abs.html)
- A companion handout by Brawand, Johnson & Kolvites (CEC 2015, "Fostering Success in Mathematics for Students with VI") maps each tool to specific math skills: abacus, braille/Nemeth, tactile graphics and manipulatives. — [Paths to Literacy PDF](https://www.pathstoliteracy.org/sites/pathstoliteracy.perkinsdev1.org/files/MathematicsSkills_TOOLS_3.25.15.pdf); [Paths to Literacy resource page](https://www.pathstoliteracy.org/resource/alignment-mathematical-skills-instructional-tools-students-visual-impairments/)
- Klingenberg, Holkesvik & Augestad (2019), *Cogent Education* 6, "Research evidence for mathematics education for students with visual impairment: A systematic review." Scope: peer-reviewed English articles from 2000–2017, searched in SCOPUS, PubMed, ERIC and Web of Science, with quality rated by the QATSDD tool. **11 publications** met the criteria, covering teacher attitudes and experiences, the abacus, tactile graphics and the development of mathematical concepts. Main conclusion: students with VI without cognitive disabilities can be expected to keep pace with their grade level in math. The authors also call for high-quality RCTs. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/ee24430506bb4cad9982c0d30838b6bd); [UiTM open-access record](https://openaccess.library.uitm.edu.my/Record/doaj-ee24430506bb4cad9982c0d30838b6bd); [NTNU Open](https://ntnuopen.ntnu.no/ntnu-xmlui/handle/11250/2600803); [VI Insight Hub summary](https://www.vihub.org/research-article/research-evidence-for-mathematics-education-for-students-with-visual-impairment-a-systematic-review)
- A secondary source summarising Klingenberg et al. (2019) reports their claim that tactile learners "need additional time and direct instruction to learn specific skills." This could not be verified in the primary text. — [arXiv 2202.12280 (Tactile Materials in Practice)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.12280)
- Klingenberg et al. (2019/2020), *British Journal of Visual Impairment*, "Digital learning in mathematics for students with severe VI: A systematic review" (Jan 2000–Nov 2017). It found "a lack of evidence for how digital technologies improve inclusion potentials and learning in mathematics for students with VI." The number of included studies is reported as **13** by one source and as "11 studies of moderate quality" by another secondary source. This conflict is unresolved. — [NTNU Open](https://ntnuopen.ntnu.no/ntnu-xmlui/handle/11250/2626773); [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/336050095_Digital_learning_in_mathematics_for_students_with_severe_visual_impairment_A_systematic_review)
- Zhang (2016), "A Review of Literature: Mathematics Instruction for Students with Visual Impairments," *iMedPub J. Childhood & Developmental Disorders* 2(1):2 (a low-prestige venue). A systematic search found **only 5 studies, all single-case designs published after 2010**. The author attributes the small number to the difficulty of recruiting participants with VI. The interventions included the VISO (voice input, speech output) calculator compared with a talking calculator or a human entering numbers: students were slower with VISO at first but gained independence. All three early studies used audio material, yet "none of the audio technologies were clearly more effective than traditional methods." — [iMedPub HTML](https://childhood-developmental-disorders.imedpub.com/articles/a-review-of-literaturemathematics-instruction-for-students-with-visual-impairments.php?aid=7864); [iMedPub PDF](https://childhood-developmental-disorders.imedpub.com/articles/a-review-of-literaturemathematics-instruction-for-students-with-visual-impairments.pdf)
- VISO calculator primary study: "Speaking Math — A Voice Input, Speech Output Calculator for Students with Visual Impairments." — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/286637851_Speaking_Math_-_A_Voice_Input_Speech_Output_Calculator_for_Students_with_Visual_Impairments)

**Abacus**
- The evidence for the abacus is mixed and dated. Nolan & Morris (1964) documented higher math achievement after abacus training, but Kapperman (1974) found braille and mental calculation **more accurate** than the abacus. One review concludes that evidence for abacus instruction "must be considered limited." — [iMedPub review](https://childhood-developmental-disorders.imedpub.com/articles/a-review-of-literaturemathematics-instruction-for-students-with-visual-impairments.php?aid=7864)
- The abacus is described as useful for its "speed, accuracy, portability, and flexibility" (Kapperman, Heinze & Sticken, 1997). This is a descriptive claim, not a controlled comparison. — [NFB JBIR Brawand & Johnson](https://nfb.org/images/nfb/publications/jbir/jbir16/jbir060101.html)
- Students with VI tend to stop using the abacus after about grade 5 and prefer the **talking calculator in high school** (Amato et al., 2013, as cited in a review). — cited via search summary of [Brawand & Johnson, JBIR](https://nfb.org/images/nfb/publications/jbir/jbir16/jbir060101.html)
- TVI preparation is weak: in an abacus teacher survey, 26.8% (45 teachers) were unsure about their own experience teaching the abacus. — [ERIC EJ1017593 (The Abacus: Teachers' Preparation and Beliefs)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1017593.pdf); see also [Instruction by TVIs, ERIC EJ1017555](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1017555.pdf)

**Nemeth code**
- No study directly measuring outcomes of early Nemeth instruction was found. A Kapperman-coauthored web-based Nemeth tutorial reportedly produced "statistically significant growth scores" compared with a matched group that did not use it. The design and N were not verified. — [JBIR 2016 Nemeth tutorial technical paper](https://nfb.org/images/nfb/publications/jbir/jbir16/jbir060103.html); [ResearchGate: Web-based Tutorial for Nemeth Code](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/329640019_A_Web-based_Tutorial_for_the_Nemeth_Braille_Code_for_Mathematics_and_Science_Notation)
- TVIs lack Nemeth preparation: a 2003 JVIB article and a 2004 survey of 135 TVIs. — [ERIC EJ662830](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ662830); [ERIC EJ683816 PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ683816.pdf)

**Explicit / systematic instruction (VI-specific)**
- Rosenblum, Cheng & Beal (2018), JVIB 112, 475–487, ran focus groups with 11 TVIs. Teachers stressed individualizing instruction, teaching a **systematic approach**, and making sure graphics are clear. The authors' conclusion: "From an early age, visually impaired students need explicit instruction in how to access information in graphics. The use of prescribed low vision devices, manipulatives, and systematic instruction are integral to student success." — [ERIC EJ1192227 PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1192227.pdf); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Teachers-of-Students-with-Visual-Impairments-Share-Rosenblum-Cheng/34c3a233d45900c1ebf52659f86bd3b1d6c12446); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/328031720_Teachers_of_Students_with_Visual_Impairments_Share_Experiences_and_Advice_for_Supporting_Students_in_Understanding_Graphics)
- A later paper summarises the TVIs' strategies as systematically familiarizing the student with the graphic, connecting the graphic to the student's experiences, and teaching skills with practice opportunities. — [JVIB 2021, "Experiences Building Graphics Literacy Skills" (SAGE)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0145482X211016918)
- General special-education evidence, from LD rather than VI samples: explicit, step-by-step, problem-specific math instruction produced increased gains. This is indirect evidence for VI. — [ERIC ED521890](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED521890.pdf)
- Modified schema-based instruction (MSBI), from moderate/severe disability samples rather than VI, had an overall effect of Tau = 1.0 in a review of 12 studies, yet "is not yet an EBP." It is a reference point for word-problem schemas. — [Univ. of Kentucky Scholars](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/a-systematic-review-of-modified-schema-based-instruction-for-teac/)

**Computer and audio-assisted instruction (secondary-relevant)**
- AnimalWatch-VI (Beal, Rosenblum & Smith, 2011 pilot) was a self-voicing prealgebra word-problem tutor with spoken hints, tested with 14 students in grades 5–12. Students scored over 90% correct on easy and medium problems and about 50% on hard ones (snippet). The audio hints seemed to help students below grade level most. — [ResearchGate pilot](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/265401112_A_Pilot_Study_of_a_Self-voicing_Computer_Program_for_Prealgebra_Math_Problems); [iMedPub review](https://childhood-developmental-disorders.imedpub.com/articles/a-review-of-literaturemathematics-instruction-for-students-with-visual-impairments.php?aid=7864)
- A national evaluation followed (Beal & Rosenblum, 2018, JVIB 112(1), 5–19). Perkins describes its results as positive. The current app has 24 units of six word problems each, with audio feedback and hints. The IES grant covers 14 modules (computation, fractions, variables) that combine self-voicing with braille and tactile graphics. — [Perkins](https://www.perkins.org/resource/apps-students-build-math-word-problem-solving-skills-and-graphics-literacy-skills/); [IES award](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/animalwatch-vi-suite-comprehensive-program-increase-access-mathematics-students-visual-impairments?ID=1259); [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/animalwatch-vi-suite/id1051014170)
- Tuttle & Carter (2023) published a systematic review of computer-assisted instruction for students with VI in the *Journal of Special Education Technology*. Its findings were not retrieved. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/01626434221088026)

**Peer tutoring**
- No VI-specific math peer-tutoring study was found in the reviews that were retrievable (see Gaps).

### Inferences
- "Evidence-based" for VI math today means converging small-N studies plus expert consensus, not effect-size-backed practices. The report should say this honestly.
- The best-supported instructional principles are explicit, systematic, sequenced instruction and multimodal access (braille/Nemeth plus tactile plus audio). They are the principles that transfer most directly to a voice assistant. Device-specific findings, for the abacus and Nemeth, are less relevant to an audio-only agent but show that the student normally relies on non-audio tools too.
- Because high-schoolers shift to talking calculators, an audio assistant fits secondary-age habits. It should support the student's own scratch work (braille/Nemeth notes, a talking calculator) and not replace it.

### Gaps
- No pooled effect sizes (d, g, Tau, PND) for any VI-specific math intervention could be verified. The full texts of Klingenberg (2019) and Zhang (2016) were not accessible.
- Kapperman's and Rosenblum's VI-specific explicit-instruction single-case studies (e.g., with multiple-baseline designs) were not located through search.
- No study of peer tutoring for math with blind or low-vision secondary students was found.
- A 2009 schema-based word-problem study with 3 students with VI (grades 3–4) appeared only in an unattributed snippet. Its source could not be confirmed, so it is not cited above.

## 2. How do expert TVIs and readers verbally present math problems? Documented verbal strategies

### Takeaway
There is no single published TVI standard for speaking math, but converging guidance exists. The listener needs explicit, unambiguous boundary words ("start fraction … end fraction", "end quantity"), consistent and simple technical terms, and chunking of long expressions into vocabulary-defined pieces. A reader-scribe who reads each step aloud let one blind student succeed through college algebra and pre-calculus (Process-Driven Math). TVIs' own descriptions of graphics often do not match accessibility guidelines.

### Cited Findings
- **ETS Guidelines for Test Readers.** Math should be read with care because the listener "has no visual reference." The reader should silently read the whole question before reading it aloud. Say "end fraction" whenever there is doubt where a fraction stops, and announce where enclosed portions end ("end quantity"). Use "technically correct yet simple terms" and be **consistent** across similar expressions. — [ETS Test Reader Guidelines](https://www.ets.org/disabilities/resources/test-reader-guidelines.html); [ETS PDF](https://www.eu.ets.org/pdfs/disabilities/guidelines-for-test-reader.pdf)
- Neil Soiffer (MathPlayer developer) says there is no standard way to speak math. For blind listeners who are not subject experts, the speaker should mark the beginning and end of fractions and scripts, e.g., "start fraction a+1 over 2 end fraction." This is a W3C mailing-list post, so it is expert opinion. — [W3C wai-xtech archive](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/wai-xtech/2012Jun/0012.html)
- **Process-Driven Math** (Gulley, Smith, Price, Prickett & Ragland, JVIB 111(5), 465–471, 2017) is an auditory method built around **chunking**. Expressions are broken into pieces named by math vocabulary, and a human reader-scribe reads each step aloud, which the authors argue eases load on working memory. With it, one blind student, who was also mobility-impaired and could not speak above a whisper, passed college algebra and pre-calculus with trigonometry. This is a single case and a "Practice Perspective," not a controlled trial. — [ERIC EJ1156239](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1156239); [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1156239.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/320432160_Process-Driven_Math_An_Auditory_Method_of_Mathematics_Instruction_and_Assessment_for_Students_who_Are_Blind_or_Have_Low_Vision)
- A follow-up user-centered evaluation of Process-Driven Math involved two college students, one blind and one with low vision (Gulley dissertation, Auburn 2021). The method needs substantial training for both student and reader-scribe, and the authors suggest that software working the same way could remove the need for a human scribe. — [Auburn dissertation](https://etd.auburn.edu/bitstream/handle/10415/7656/Dissertation_Ann_Gulley_Auburn_Spring_2021.pdf?sequence=2&isAllowed=y); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Process-Driven-Math:-An-Auditory-Method-of-and-for-Gulley-Smith/f7cbb40435cb1ad22cb2d3a70b719b01e97f1914)
- **Teachers' descriptions of graphics** (Rosenblum, Cheng, Zebehazy, Emerson & Beal, JVIB 2020). TVIs described 12 math graphics for two hypothetical students, a braille reader and a print reader with low vision. Their descriptions were compared with NCAM description guidelines. The authors conclude that teachers need better preparation to make graphics accessible and to support students' interpretation of graphics, which implies TVI descriptions often diverged from the guidelines. — [ERIC EJ1255993 record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1255993); [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1255993.pdf)
- TVIs recommend verbally describing math content that contains visual images, offering multiple formats so students can choose, and teaching braille readers a systematic two-handed exploration of tactile graphics (Rosenblum, Cheng & Beal, 2018). — [TTAC Online, Math Instructional Strategies for Students who are Blind/VI](https://ttaconline.org/differentiated-instructional-strategies-visual-impairments)
- Students themselves ask for strategies. In Rosenblum & Herzberg (2015, JVIB 109(3), 173–184), 12 students in grades 6–12 said a common strategy was to ask a teacher for a verbal description or for the graphic to be explained another way. "Good" tactile graphics were defined by "clarity of information." Students often lacked materials at the same time as their peers, and almost none had been asked for input on tactile graphic design. — [ERIC EJ1114436 PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1114436.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/283521779_Braille_and_Tactile_Graphics_Youths_with_Visual_Impairments_Share_Their_Experiences); [NCEO reference](https://nceo.info/references/article-journal/12806)
- Spoken math is ambiguous because equations read aloud can be interpreted in more than one way. — [JBIR 2019, Access to Math and Science Content for Youth Who Are Blind or VI](https://nfb.org/images/nfb/publications/jbir/jbir19/jbir090101.html) (via search summary)

### Inferences
- Verbal rules a voice assistant should follow:
  1. Preview the whole problem internally, then give an overview first, e.g., "This is an equation with a fraction on the left and two terms on the right."
  2. Mark every structural boundary explicitly: "start fraction / end fraction", "open parenthesis / close parenthesis" or "quantity / end quantity", "superscript … back to baseline", "square root of … end root".
  3. Use one consistent term per symbol for the whole session.
  4. Chunk long expressions by named math units, as Process-Driven Math does, and confirm each chunk before moving on.
  5. Offer re-reads and step-by-step navigation on request.
  6. Explain each step as a think-aloud ("Now I multiply both sides by 3 because …"), mirroring the explicit-instruction and reader-scribe models.
- For a Turkish-language assistant, the boundary vocabulary should be fixed in Turkish (e.g., "kesir başı … kesir sonu", "parantez aç / parantez kapa", "üssü … üs sonu", "karekök içi … karekök sonu"). This is a design inference, not a sourced standard.

### Gaps
- No empirical study was found that compares specific TVI verbal techniques, such as think-aloud versus no think-aloud or checking understanding versus not, with blind secondary students. The guidance is consensus and practice, not trial evidence.
- No published TVI-specific standard for spoken math was found. ETS reader rules come from test accommodations.
- No source was found on optimal pacing or speech rate for spoken math with blind students. The full texts of the ETS reports may contain it.

## 3. Tactile graphics and manipulatives vs. purely verbal explanation: when is verbal alone insufficient?

### Takeaway
For short, linear expressions, verbal presentation works roughly as well as tactile presentation. For spatial content (graphs, geometry, maps, multi-level or nested expressions), combining tactile and verbal input beats verbal alone, and verbal-only presentation becomes cognitively overloading as structures get longer and nested. TVIs and students favor tactile graphics plus verbal description, not either one alone.

### Cited Findings
- **Riga & Kouroupetroglou (2024)**, *Multimodal Technologies and Interaction* 8(7):57, studied blind participants' recall of math expressions in auditory, tactile and auditory–tactile modalities. Total errors (snippet): **2403 auditory, 1606 tactile, 1399 auditory–tactile**. Errors rose with complexity and rose significantly more in the auditory modality. For **low-complexity expressions (up to about 10 items)** performance was similar across modalities. For medium complexity, tactile and audio-tactile input were better. Tactile reading took much longer than listening. The authors report a short-term-memory overload threshold tied to the type and number of elements in an expression. — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2414-4088/8/7/57); [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/9e8d/57260f7579c4e7c015533d31336d6ef3b60d.pdf)
- **Tactile Data Comics** (Jiao, Sun et al., ACM ASSETS 2025) tested 16 blind students on chart comprehension. Accuracy was 70.1% for verbal narration alone, 70.1% for verbal narration plus static tactile graphics, and **76.6%** for verbal narration plus step-by-step tactile "data comics." The gain came from the stepwise presentation, not from tactile graphics alone. Students with less tactile-graphics experience relied more on verbal narration. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3663547.3746338); [SMU repository](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/sis_research/10704/)
- **Tactile charts (2025)**: tactile input may not significantly improve immediate basic comprehension, but it supports retaining and applying knowledge of chart types in more complex or long-term tasks. — [arXiv 2507.21462](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21462v2)
- **MAIDR (2024)**: relying on the verbal channel alone "might be cognitively overloading when it gets longer and nested." — [arXiv 2403.00717](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.00717)
- In spatial tasks, blind participants performed significantly better after reading an audio-tactile map than after hearing a verbal description. This is a wayfinding study, not math, so it is analogous evidence only. — [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-40238-3_55); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/316740490_Orientation_and_mobility_aids_for_individuals_with_blindness_Verbal_description_vs_audio-tactile_map)
- A high-school graph-access dissertation (snippet) reports that teachers think instructing students with VI "entails more than solely providing … tactile graphics or verbal descriptions" and that students access graphs in tactile form rather than through sound or verbal descriptions. — referenced in search results for [Rosenblum/Herzberg query; see TTAC](https://ttaconline.org/differentiated-instructional-strategies-visual-impairments) (primary dissertation URL not recovered; treat as unverified)
- TVIs describe low vision devices, **manipulatives** and systematic instruction as "integral to student success" (Rosenblum, Cheng & Beal, 2018). — [ERIC EJ1192227](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1192227.pdf)
- Brawand & Johnson (2016) recommend tactile and concrete materials as part of the best combination. — [NFB JBIR](https://nfb.org/images/nfb/publications/jbir/jbir16/jbir060101abs.html)
- A practitioner guide argues tactile graphics are especially important for math, science and geography content and less effective for complex images. — [Paths to Literacy, Tactile Graphics PDF](https://www.pathstoliteracy.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/Tactile_Graphics.pdf)
- Virtual and concrete manipulatives improve math outcomes for secondary students with disabilities. A review of 19 single-case studies covered learning disabilities, not VI, so the evidence is indirect, and virtual manipulatives are visual by default. — [ResearchGate Bouck & Flanagan](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/341506215_Virtual_manipulatives_as_assistive_technology_to_support_students_with_disabilities_with_mathematics); [Systematic Review of Manipulatives](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/324155860_A_Systematic_Review_of_the_Literature_on_Mathematics_Manipulatives_to_Support_Students_with_Disabilities)

### Inferences
- Where verbal alone is likely insufficient: graphs and coordinate planes, geometry figures, tables, number lines, and expressions with more than about 10 elements or with nesting (stacked fractions, exponents in fractions, nested parentheses).
- Design rules for an audio-only assistant:
  1. Estimate an expression's complexity and, above a threshold, switch to chunked, navigable delivery, e.g., "The numerator has 3 parts. Shall I read the first one?"
  2. Describe figures structurally: give the type first, then axes, then key points, then the trend, following NCAM-style descriptions.
  3. Explicitly suggest a tactile or concrete aid when the content is spatial, e.g., "If you have a tactile graph sheet or graph paper with raised lines, mark these points…"
  4. Narrate step by step. The tactile data comics result suggests that stepwise sequencing itself adds about 6.5 percentage points.
  5. Offload memory: restate the current state of the problem after each step instead of relying on the student to hold the whole expression.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found that compares a verbal-only tutor with a verbal-plus-tactile tutor for secondary algebra or geometry with blind students.
- The exact complexity threshold and statistics in Riga & Kouroupetroglou (2024) come from snippets. The full text was not read.

## 4. What does research say about auditory/speech presentation of math (TTS, recorded audio) for blind students' comprehension and accuracy?

### Takeaway
Well-designed synthesized speech of algebra-level math gives secondary blind and low-vision students about the same accuracy as their usual braille or print (ETS, N = 21 per arm). The speech style matters: ClearSpeak beat MathSpeak and SimpleSpeak. Extra prosody (pauses) did not clearly help, but lexical cues did. Auditory recall degrades fastest as complexity rises, and digital-audio textbooks led to slower work and somewhat worse algebra solving than traditional formats in a 3-student study.

### Cited Findings
- **ETS "Expanding Audio Access to Mathematics Expressions…via MathML"** (Frankel, Brownstein et al., ETS RR-17-13, 2017) covered secondary-school, algebra-level math. The spoken group had 21 students (8 blind, 13 low vision). The comparison group was 8 braille users and 13 print users. The finding: "little observable difference existed between students' success in answering math and math-parsing questions when using the spoken math tools and their success in answering parallel questions posed in braille or print." Teacher authoring tools were easy to use. — [ETS](https://ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2017/jxpm.html); [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ets2.12132); [ERIC EJ1168599](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1168599)
- **ClearSpeak** (Frankel et al., ETS 2016) had mean scores "notably higher for ClearSpeak than for SimpleSpeak or MathSpeak" for all students and for both blind and low-vision subgroups. Blind students scored higher than students with low vision, and the gap was smallest under ClearSpeak. — [Wiley ETS RR](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ets2.12103); [ERIC ED570857](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED570857.pdf)
- **Prosodic vs. lexical cues** (Frankel & Brownstein, ETS RR-16-33, 2016; N = 15, 7 blind and 8 low vision, 8 questions per style). The prosodic enhancements, mainly pauses, "did not appear to improve comprehension," while "certain lexical cues did appear to have some value." — [ETS](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2016/jwvb.html); [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ets2.12119); [ERIC ED570858](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED570858)
- **Speech vs. braille vs. large print** (ICCHP 2016). Students using NVDA with MathPlayer reached comprehension rates similar to those in their preferred modality. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/304822065_A_Study_of_Speech_Versus_Braille_and_Large_Print_of_Mathematical_Expressions)
- **EAR-Math** (Kacorri, Riga & Kouroupetroglou, 2014), a pilot with 5 sighted and 2 blind participants and 39 Greek MathPlayer stimuli. Structural elements had the highest error rates. Errors fell from 18% on the first attempt to 10% and then 7% on later attempts, which shows that listeners improve with repetition and familiarity. — [UMD PDF](https://terpconnect.umd.edu/~hernisa/pub/2014-uahci-kacorri-riga-kouroupetroglou.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/262731825_EAR-Math_Evaluation_of_Audio_Rendered_Mathematics)
- **Auditory recall** is worst for complex expressions (Riga & Kouroupetroglou, 2024; see Q3). — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2414-4088/8/7/57)
- **Bouck & Weng (2014)**, "Reading Math: A Comparison of Reading and Listening to Algebraic Problems," with 3 secondary students with VI. Students tended to solve algebra better with the traditional textbook than with the digital or audio one. Task completion took longer with the digital textbook for all 3, and 2 of 3 preferred the traditional textbook. — [SAGE JSET](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/016264341402900401)
- **Bouck, Meyer, Joshi & Schleppenbach (2013)**, "Accessing algebra via MathSpeak: Potential and pitfalls…," *JSET* 28(1), 49–62. A small qualitative eText study. Its findings were not retrieved beyond the title framing of "potential and pitfalls." — referenced via [SAGE Bouck & Meyer 2012 eText](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/004005991204500206) and [MathSpeak paper, ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/3272151.3272152)
- MathSpeak was designed as a "non-ambiguous language for audio rendering of MathML." — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/3272151.3272152)
- **Indonesian qualitative study**: the audio representation of math significantly affected students' understanding, especially for mixed fractions and multi-level expressions. The authors call for standardized audio representation. — [JPMat (Unimus)](https://jurnal.unimus.ac.id/index.php/JPMat/article/view/20405)
- AnimalWatch-VI's self-voicing hints helped students below grade level (see Q1). One commentary reports that VoiceOver was hard to understand or inconsistent in this context. — [iMedPub](https://childhood-developmental-disorders.imedpub.com/articles/a-review-of-literaturemathematics-instruction-for-students-with-visual-impairments.php?aid=7864); [emilyshank.work](https://emilyshank.work/articles)
- Screen readers read math correctly only when it is encoded as MathML or live text. Images of equations are inaccessible. — [UW DO-IT](https://www.washington.edu/doit/node/4761); [Rutgers RADR](https://radr.rutgers.edu/resource/making-math-accessible)

### Inferences
- Audio-only delivery is viable for secondary algebra-level content, with accuracy comparable to braille or print, if the verbalization is well designed, meaning ClearSpeak-like. ClearSpeak's principle is natural, human-teacher phrasing with lexical boundary cues as needed, and less verbosity for simple cases (e.g., "x squared", "one half"), with explicit "start/end" only when the structure is ambiguous.
- Do not rely on pauses or intonation alone to convey structure. Use words, since lexical cues helped and prosody did not.
- Expect a learning curve: errors dropped from 18% to 7% with repetition in EAR-Math. Keep the verbalization scheme stable so the student learns it, and offer a short "how I read math" orientation.
- Audio can be slower and more effortful than braille or print (Bouck & Weng). Give the student control: repeat, slow down, "read only the numerator", "where am I?".
- The assistant should parse problems into a structured representation (MathML or LaTeX) before speaking, and never read raw OCR or LaTeX symbols.

### Gaps
- No large RCT on TTS for math with blind secondary students was found. The largest sample is ETS's N = 21 per arm.
- No Turkish-language research on spoken math rendering for blind students was found in this search. Standardizing Turkish math speech remains an open design problem.
- Effect sizes and the per-item results of the ETS ClearSpeak and prosody studies were not retrieved, since the full texts were blocked.
- No evidence was found on recorded human audio versus TTS for math comprehension.
