## 1. Verification of the key studies: methods, samples, numbers and conclusions

### Takeaway
The full texts confirm that the evidence base for teaching math to students with visual impairment (VI) is thin, mostly small-N, and has no pooled effect sizes:
- Klingenberg 2019 has 11 studies.
- The digital-learning review has 13 studies (not 11).
- Zhang 2016 has 5 single-case studies.

Spoken math built from structured MathML (ClearSpeak with interactive navigation) gave secondary blind and low-vision students essentially the same accuracy as braille or print: mean item score 0.762 spoken vs 0.756 non-spoken, N = 21, within-subject.

Purely auditory recall breaks down from about 11 elements of expression complexity onward. Two sample corrections:
- The Riga study used young adults (mean age about 21), not secondary students.
- The Tactile Data Comics study used college students.

The step-by-step tactile gain in Tactile Data Comics (76.6% vs 70.1%) did **not** show a subject-by-condition interaction for math.

### Cited Findings

**Klingenberg, Holkesvik & Augestad (2019), *Cogent Education* 6:1626322 — [TAM METİN]**
- **Design.** Systematic review of peer-reviewed English articles, 1 Jan 2000 to 31 Oct 2017, searched in SCOPUS, PubMed, ERIC and Web of Science. Ages 5–25 were included. Studies of students with VI plus comorbidity or multiple disabilities were excluded.
- **Study flow.** 278 abstracts → 108 → 53 after 55 duplicates were removed → 25 relevant. These 25 were "divide[d] … into two separate groups according to length." The math review uses **11**; the rest went to the separate digital-learning paper (pp. 5–6). — [NTNU Open](https://hdl.handle.net/11250/2600803); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/2331186X.2019.1626322)
- **Quality.** Rated with QATSDD (14 quantitative items, maximum 42; >75% = high). Results: "four studies were classified as 'high quality', six as 'good quality', and one study as 'moderate quality'." Designs: 7 observational, 2 interview, 2 mixed methods including an experiment (p. 6). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/2331186X.2019.1626322)
- **Abstract quote:** "The authors conclude that it is to be expected that students with VI but without cognitive disabilities will follow their grade level in mathematics. The findings highlight the need for more randomly controlled, high quality trials" (pp. 1–2). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/2331186X.2019.1626322)
- **Previously unverified claim, now verified (section 4.3, p. 14).** "The use of graphics is one example of a curriculum theme for which students who are tactile readers may need additional time and direct instruction in order to learn specific skills and strategies (Rosenblum & Herzberg, 2015; Zebehazy & Wilton, 2014). Such students need accurate information and specific feedback about their skills to know whether they have completed a task accurately." This is the reviewers' synthesis of two primary studies, not a new finding. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/2331186X.2019.1626322)
- **Clearest advice from the review (p. 14):** "The least ambiguous advice that emerged from our review is that the competence and attitudes of teachers improve significantly when they reflect on their own practice together with a supervisor." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/2331186X.2019.1626322)
- **Realistic expectations (p. 14).**
  - In a 40-year Norwegian braille-student sample (n = 248), 57% were taught math at grade level.
  - In Rule et al. (2011), "10 out of 12 were as successful in mathematics as their peers" when given appropriate materials. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/2331186X.2019.1626322)
- **Error in the source.** The caption of Table 1 reads "Characteristics of the 11 studies of digital mathematics learning," which looks like a copy error from the companion paper (p. 7). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/2331186X.2019.1626322)

**Klingenberg, Holkesvik & Augestad, "Digital learning in mathematics for students with severe visual impairment: A systematic review" (*British Journal of Visual Impairment*; accepted manuscript on NTNU Open) — [TAM METİN]**
- **DÜZELTME / conflict resolved.** The review includes **13** publications, not 11: "Our review of digital learning in mathematics for students with severe VI is based on 13 publications (Figure 1), while the other 12 publications will be presented in a separate article." The earlier secondary claim of "11 studies of moderate quality" is wrong. The quality ratings were "three articles were classified as 'high quality', the other ten articles as 'good quality'." — [NTNU Open](https://hdl.handle.net/11250/2626773)
- **Inconsistency in the source.** 13 + "other 12" = 25, but the companion math review says it used 11 of the same 25. The NTNU abstract also says "Eight papers reported the use of audio-based applications," while the body says "audio was applied in 9 of the 13 studies." — [NTNU Open](https://hdl.handle.net/11250/2626773)
- **Samples.** "The number of students with VI … varies from 3 to 16. They ranged in age from 6 years to 55 years (mean age 19 years), and their mathematical training lasted from one session to 18 weeks." Twelve studies were intervention or experimental, one cross-sectional. — [NTNU Open](https://hdl.handle.net/11250/2626773)
- **On audio, the authors are cautious:**
  - "The conclusions from the 9 studies are not unambiguous in terms of learning effects, nor are student attitudes to using auditory information in their learning."
  - "any suggestion that audio may be an alternative to Braille should be regarded with caution."
  - "students with VI are more successful if they can read traditional texts and mathematical notations by themselves when learning algebra, whereas just listening to digital texts makes the learning process harder." This last point is based on the authors' own teaching experience. — [NTNU Open](https://hdl.handle.net/11250/2626773)

**Zhang (2016), *J. Childhood & Developmental Disorders* 2(1):2 — [TAM METİN]**
- **Search.** ERIC, EBSCOhost, Google Scholar, PsycInfo and ProQuest, plus hand-searches of 6 journals. Inclusion followed What Works Clearinghouse (WWC) criteria: experimental, quasi-experimental or single-subject designs in peer-reviewed English journals. "The electronic search yielded 273 results and four … met inclusionary criteria. The hand-search identified another article." Five studies in total, all single-case, all published 2011–2014. — [iMedPub](https://childhood-developmental-disorders.imedpub.com/articles/a-review-of-literaturemathematics-instruction-for-students-with-visual-impairments.php?aid=7864)
- **Audio technology (3 studies).** "All three studies provided audio material … However, none of the three studies could make a strong conclusion that assistive technologies providing audio information are more effective than traditional methods." — [iMedPub](https://childhood-developmental-disorders.imedpub.com/articles/a-review-of-literaturemathematics-instruction-for-students-with-visual-impairments.php?aid=7864)
- **Design critique.**
  - Beal et al. (2011) had "no baseline session."
  - Chang & Bin (2013) was a single-participant AB design.
  - The Bouck alternating-treatment designs "could not establish a functional relation." — [iMedPub](https://childhood-developmental-disorders.imedpub.com/articles/a-review-of-literaturemathematics-instruction-for-students-with-visual-impairments.php?aid=7864)
- **VISO calculator (Bouck et al., 2011).** 3 students aged 18–19, so post-secondary age. With VISO, students "required more time … [and] a higher number of attempts." Both decreased with experience but stayed above their usual method. The free speech recognizer "sometimes was unable to understand what the students were saying." Students valued the independence it gave them. — [iMedPub](https://childhood-developmental-disorders.imedpub.com/articles/a-review-of-literaturemathematics-instruction-for-students-with-visual-impairments.php?aid=7864)

**Bouck & Weng (2014), *JSET* 29(4) — [ÖZET], plus Zhang's full-text summary**
- **Design.** Alternating-treatment single-subject design with 3 students with VI in grades 9–12, comparing a digital textbook to a traditional one. The digital version was the "ReadHear" supported eText player with MathSpeak output. The traditional textbook came in small print (one student used a CCTV) or braille. Measures were points per assessment on Glencoe Algebra I items and completion time. — [iMedPub (Zhang)](https://childhood-developmental-disorders.imedpub.com/articles/a-review-of-literaturemathematics-instruction-for-students-with-visual-impairments.php?aid=7864); [SAGE abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/016264341402900401)
- **Results.**
  - Students "solved the algebra equations better when presented via their traditional textbook."
  - Completion took longer with the digital text for all 3.
  - 2 of 3 preferred the traditional textbook. No effect sizes were reported. — [iMedPub (Zhang)](https://childhood-developmental-disorders.imedpub.com/articles/a-review-of-literaturemathematics-instruction-for-students-with-visual-impairments.php?aid=7864)
- **Conflict.** The PDF-extracted Table 1 of the Klingenberg digital review places "5 VI, age 16–19, Algebra 1 or 2, 2 teachers" near the Bouck & Weng row. This is probably a column misalignment with Bouck, Weng & Satsangi (2016). Treat it as unreliable. — [NTNU Open](https://hdl.handle.net/11250/2626773)

**Brawand & Johnson (2016), *JBIR* 6(1) — [TAM METİN]**
- **Nature of the paper.** A narrative "summary of literature." It has no search protocol, no inclusion criteria and no effect sizes, so it should not be cited as a systematic review. — [NFB JBIR](https://nfb.org/images/nfb/publications/jbir/jbir16/jbir060101.html)
- **Previously only via snippet, now verified.** "students with visual impairments tend not to use the abacus after fifth grade, and prefer to use the talking calculator in high school (Amato et al., 2013)." — [NFB JBIR](https://nfb.org/images/nfb/publications/jbir/jbir16/jbir060101.html)
- **Relevant to verbal instruction.** The "logic-partner" abacus method "focuses on understanding the 'what' and 'why' of the steps in solving a problem … using synthesis and verbalizing." — [NFB JBIR](https://nfb.org/images/nfb/publications/jbir/jbir16/jbir060101.html)
- **Multimodal sequence (citing Osterhaus, 1996).** "students with visual impairments should read a mathematical problem, write it, listen to it being read, tactually explore the problem through manipulatives, and if possible move their body and/or manipulative through space." — [NFB JBIR](https://nfb.org/images/nfb/publications/jbir/jbir16/jbir060101.html)
- **Advanced symbols.** TVIs "are not expected to know the meaning of each print symbol, but should consult with the general education teacher to ensure that the symbols have been correctly interpreted." — [NFB JBIR](https://nfb.org/images/nfb/publications/jbir/jbir16/jbir060101.html)

**Rosenblum, Cheng & Beal (2018), *JVIB* 112(5), 475–487 — [TAM METİN]** (details under Question 2)
- **Sample and procedure.** 11 TVIs, all female and White: 9 itinerant teachers and 2 who taught junior-high and high-school math at schools for the blind. Experience ranged 4–25 years (median 7, mean 13). There were 3 Zoom focus groups (4, 4 and 3 people), each lasting 60–80 minutes, with answer order rotated. Analysis was inductive and deductive coding into **12 themes**, with only 2 coder disagreements (pp. 477–478). — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1192227.pdf)

**Gulley et al. (2017), Process-Driven Math, *JVIB* 111(5), 465–471 — [TAM METİN]** (details under Questions 2 and 3)
- This is a "Practice Perspective," not a controlled study. The method was built for one blind, mobility-impaired student who could only whisper and who "lacked the sensitivity in his fingers" for braille. He "successfully solved well over one thousand mathematics problem[s]" and passed college algebra and pre-calculus with trigonometry. A second student with severe VI, who had never been taught braille, also completed college algebra (pp. 465, 469). — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1156239.pdf)
- **DÜZELTME.** The earlier notes said the method uses a reader-scribe who "reads each step aloud." The method's core is different. The reader-scribe "systematically reveals the algebraic expression in layers," hiding the symbols behind math vocabulary. **The student** directs every transformation: "places control over the flow of information into the hands of the student" (p. 465). — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1156239.pdf)

**Riga & Kouroupetroglou (2024), *MTI* 8(7):57 — [TAM METİN]**
- **Participants.** 16 blind participants, 8 male and 8 female, aged **21.25 ± 5.98 years** with 13.27 ± 3.86 years of education. All had 95–100% vision loss and used braille, all were Greek-speaking, and they had attended inclusive secondary school. The stimuli covered concepts "taught as part of the Greek secondary school curriculum" (pp. 3–4).
  - **DÜZELTME:** the participants were mostly adults, not secondary students. — [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/9e8d/57260f7579c4e7c015533d31336d6ef3b60d.pdf); [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2414-4088/8/7/57)
- **Procedure.**
  - **Materials:** 25 expressions × 3 parallel sets = 75 stimuli.
  - **Presentation:** tactile stimuli were embossed in Nemeth; audio stimuli were pre-recorded with MathPlayer and the Acapela Greek voice at default rate. "The participants did not have the option to navigate in the MEs," and each stimulus was presented **once only**.
  - **Training and spacing:** participants had 1 hour of training first, and the three modality units were run on separate days.
  - **Response:** answers were written on a Perkins brailler (pp. 4–6). — [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/9e8d/57260f7579c4e7c015533d31336d6ef3b60d.pdf)
- **Error counts, verified (p. 7).** "The sixteen participants committed a total number of 5408 recall errors … 2403 errors in Auditory Modality, 1606 in Tactile Modality, and 1399 in Auditory–Tactile Modality." Deletions were the most common error type. — [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/9e8d/57260f7579c4e7c015533d31336d6ef3b60d.pdf)
- **Mean time per expression (Table 1).** Auditory **7.88 s** (range 2–20). Tactile **46.18 s** (2–370). Auditory–tactile **54.06 s**. — [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/9e8d/57260f7579c4e7c015533d31336d6ef3b60d.pdf)
- **Statistics.** A three-factor ANOVA found a modality effect, F(2,405) = 10.8, p < 0.01. Auditory errors were greater than tactile (p = 0.022) and auditory–tactile (p = 0.018). — [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/9e8d/57260f7579c4e7c015533d31336d6ef3b60d.pdf)
- **Complexity.** Complexity C = structures + identifiers/numbers + operators. Errors rise **linearly** with C: "an increase in two items in the complexity of the ME results in an increase in roughly one recall error." The slopes are auditory **0.570** (95% CI 0.512–0.628), tactile 0.449, and auditory–tactile 0.389. — [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/9e8d/57260f7579c4e7c015533d31336d6ef3b60d.pdf)
- **Exact thresholds (Table 3, p. 10).** "In expressions of low complexity (up to 10 items), the participants performed equally well in all three modalities. Starting from the medium complexity of 11 items and up to 35 items, participants performed significantly better … in Tactile and especially in Auditory–Tactile Modalities than in Auditory Modality. Finally, the participants performed equally poorly in the high-complexity expression containing 46 items."
  - Not every medium level was significant. At C = 12 and C = 19, auditory vs auditory–tactile was p = 0.088 and p = 0.321.
  - Significant levels: C = 11, 14, 15, 25, 26, 29, 32, 35 (auditory vs auditory–tactile p ≤ 0.029). — [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/9e8d/57260f7579c4e7c015533d31336d6ef3b60d.pdf)
- **Design recommendation (p. 11).** "long MEs should be given to the student in smaller parts … An automatic segmentation would be preferable in comparison to self-segmentation, as in its case it could be performed on different structural levels and not random places, thereby allowing for users to listen to complete sub-expressions. Therefore, pre-recorded audio of math is not preferable." — [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/9e8d/57260f7579c4e7c015533d31336d6ef3b60d.pdf)

- **Participants. DÜZELTME:** "We recruited N = 16 participants from a blind college … All participants were college students with prior exposure to paper-based tactile graphics." **Beal & Rosenblum (2018), *JVIB* 112(1), 5–19, AnimalWatch-VI national evaluation — [TAM METİN]** (new; directly relevant to hints and solution-on-request)
- **Participants.** 43 students with VI from 17 US states: 16 braille users, 27 print users. Grades 4–10, mostly 5–7. 30 TVIs took part. — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1172160.pdf)
- **Design.** Eight alternating prealgebra units, app vs. the student's usual literacy medium, 24 problems each. Twenty percent of the problems included graphics. — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1172160.pdf)
- **Total correct.** Students solved more problems correctly with the app (M = 21.4 vs 16.7 of 24), F(1,37) = 53.805, **d = 1.03**. 88% of students did better with the app. — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1172160.pdf)
- **Important caveat.** The app allowed **3 attempts with hints after wrong answers**. On the first attempt the app was worse: "Students solved 14.2 (59%) of app problems correctly on the first try, compared to 16.7 (69%) of paper unit problems," F(1,37) = 7.016, p < .01. The authors concede: "we do not know if hints would have helped students when they worked on paper units." — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1172160.pdf)
- **Other outcomes.**
  - Motivation was higher with the app: d = 0.55, M = 4.29 vs 3.88.
  - 78% of students preferred the app; of those, 52% cited the hints and 16% cited audio right/wrong feedback. — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1172160.pdf)

**Wall Emerson & Anderson (2018), *JVIB* 112(2), 157–168 — [TAM METİN]** (new; limits of audio description)
- **Participants.** 44 students with VI: 25 blind and 19 low vision. 39 attended schools for the blind and 5 public schools. They worked at grade levels 5 (n = 3), 8 (n = 15) and 11 (n = 26), ages 10–21. — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1175990.pdf)
- **Design.** Students listened via JAWS to replicated textbook pages and could ask for re-reading. Each image was presented at one of four description levels:
  - **control:** just the word "image"
  - **terse:** e.g., "there is a line graph"
  - **NCAM:** standard NCAM-style description
  - **extended:** fuller description — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1175990.pdf)
- **Results.** Percent correct on content questions: control 15.1%, terse 13.6%, NCAM 28.8%, extended 28.6%; χ²(3) = 17.66, p = 0.001. — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1175990.pdf)
- **By image type:**
  - number lines 41.0%
  - line graphs 32.8%
  - tables 32.0%
  - shapes 22.6%
  - bar graphs 22.2%
  - ray diagrams 19.2%
  - question-specific images 15.1%
  - maps 14.1%
  - pie charts 14.0%
  - **equations 5.7%**
- **Authors' conclusion:** "there are images for which no level of description is sufficient." — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1175990.pdf)

### Inferences
- **"Audio is as good as braille or print" holds under specific conditions only.** It held in ETS RR-17-13 with well-encoded MathML, ClearSpeak, a tutorial, and student-controlled navigation (arrow keys and zoom were the most valued). It does not hold for one-shot, non-navigable audio (Riga), and it does not hold for images described in prose (Wall Emerson & Anderson). An assistant that reads a photographed question must recreate the ETS conditions: a structured parse, repeatable and navigable chunks, and student control.
- **Practical complexity budget from Riga.** Count C = structures + identifiers/numbers + operators. At C ≤ 10, read the expression whole. At C ≥ 11, give an overview first and then deliver sub-expressions on request. Riga's adults heard each expression once without navigation, so the threshold is conservative for an interactive assistant. It is likely not lenient enough for younger or weaker students. This is an inference.
- **The AnimalWatch "d = 1.03" should not be cited as evidence that audio tutoring works.** It is the effect of retries plus hints plus audio feedback. First-try accuracy was actually lower in the app. For a no-hints design, the relevant signals are:
  - Students valued immediate right/wrong feedback.
  - The app withheld the worked solution until after 3 wrong answers or an explicit "give up."
- **Turkish context.** No source here studies Turkish speakers. Riga used Greek with MathPlayer's Greek voice, which shows that localized rule sets work. The ETS quote about "to the second" vs "squared" shows that unfamiliar verbalizations cost comprehension at first. The Turkish wording should therefore match what Turkish math teachers actually say ("x kare", "x üssü 3", "a bölü b", "kesrin payı / paydası"). This echoes the TVIs' advice to use the classroom teacher's language (see Question 2).

### Gaps
- **Bouck & Weng (2014)** full text was not accessible (SAGE). No item-level scores or effect sizes. The sample size conflict (3 in the abstract and in Zhang, vs a possible misread of 5 in the Klingenberg table) could not be checked against the primary source.
- **The Klingenberg digital review** was read as the NTNU accepted manuscript. The published BJVI version may differ, especially the 8 vs 9 audio-studies inconsistency.
- **Effect sizes.** None of the systematic reviews pooled effect sizes. The only standardized effect sizes found are AnimalWatch-VI's d values (confounded, as noted). ETS gives descriptive statistics only.
- **Turkish evidence.** No Turkish-language study of spoken math for blind students was found or read in this pass.

## 2. How expert TVIs verbalize math: transcripts and coded verbal behaviors

### Takeaway
Only one study found codes TVIs' actual spoken guidance: Rosenblum et al. 2020. Ten TVIs audio-recorded how they would talk a student through 12 math graphics. Their talk was coded into description elements, exploration suggestions and questions. On average the teachers covered only about half of the NCAM key elements (individual means 20–73%). They gave about 5 exploration suggestions per graphic. They asked very few questions, and none were higher-order.

Focus-group transcripts (Rosenblum et al. 2018) show expert TVIs:
- use a fixed, generalizable step-by-step exploration script
- model first, then fade support
- send the student back to the question to list "what we know and what we have to find"
- use the general-education teacher's vocabulary
- layer the amount of guidance per student
- give timely right/wrong feedback, because many students "don't want to move on until they've heard that they are right"

Test-reader rules (ETS) define the opposite, non-teaching register: neutral, no interpretation, no hints, repeat on request. That register maps directly onto the assistant's "read and explain without hints" mode.

### Cited Findings

**Rosenblum, Cheng, Zebehazy, Wall Emerson & Beal (2020), "Teachers' Descriptions of Mathematics Graphics for Students with Visual Impairments: A Preliminary Investigation," *JVIB* 114(3), 231–236 — [TAM METİN]**
- **Participants.** 10 TVIs, all White women: 8 itinerant and 2 at schools for the blind, with 4–25 years of experience (M = 13.5) (p. 232). — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1255993.pdf); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0145482X20923442)
- **Prompt.** For each of 12 graphics: "In guiding a student to explore this graphic, what would you say to the student? Assume it is a student who needs guidance to interpret the graphic. Audio-record yourself explaining the graphic." Each graphic was described twice, for a hypothetical braille reader and a hypothetical print reader, and recordings were transcribed (p. 232). — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1255993.pdf)
- **Coding scheme (p. 233):**
  1. **Key elements.** Inclusion of NCAM key elements, scored 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 or 1 per element. Graphics averaged 11.3 key elements (range 4–20). Example of 6 elements: "There is a bar graph titled Lengths of Five White Sharks. Five blue bars labeled A, B, C, D, and E show the lengths … A is 2.5, B is 3…"
  2. **Exploration suggestions.** Instances where the teacher "suggested a specific action that the student could take to locate or interpret information" (e.g., "locate large wedge in a circle graph, use key to find the meaning of a symbol").
  3. **Questions.** Count of questions asked.
  4. **Difficulty.** Teachers' difficulty ratings. — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1255993.pdf)
- **Results (pp. 233–234):**
  - "only about half of the key elements in the NCAM descriptions were included." Teacher means ranged **20.40% to 72.91%**, F(9,220) = 12.86, p < .001. There was no braille vs print difference.
  - Suggestions: **4.97 per graphic** (braille) and **4.47** (print), with teacher means from 2.87 to 7.04.
  - "use of questions overall was low; four teachers did not include any questions. Most (82%) of the questions were posed by 2 of the 10 teachers and appeared to be used to confirm understanding … There were no instances of what might be considered higher order questions." — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1255993.pdf)
- **Verbatim transcript, the only example of question-guided interpretation (p. 234):** "When you get to the far, left side, what number do you see? That is your latitude measurement. Are you north or south of the equator? Are you far away from the equator or very close? Now put your right pointer finger on the Chicago dot again. This time, go straight up until you get to the top of the map. Do you see a number there? Keep your finger there, and use your other hand to look to the left and right of your finger. What numbers do you see? Is your finger closer to 90 or 85, or is it somewhere in the middle?" — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1255993.pdf)
- **Awareness of guidelines.** Only 1 of 10 often referenced NCAM, 3 had reviewed it, 4 had heard of it, and 2 had never heard of it. One teacher's limitation remark: "It's extremely hard to describe without knowing the student … as well as being able to watch their reactions and adjust based on responses" (p. 235). — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1255993.pdf)

- **Step-by-step exploration script (Felicia, p. 482):** "I have a very specific step-by-step process for the student to explore a graph. Start at the top and read the title. Find the label for the x-axis. Find the y-axis on the left. Then we talk about the scale. 'Let's look at the scale and find the numbers.' Point out the dotted lines in between. Be sure the student really understands the scale from bottom to top and then goes across from left to right." — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1192227.pdf)
- **A consistent "attack strategy" (Helen, pp. 482–483):** "The kids develop a consistent strategy on how they are going to attack a graphic. - **Question → explore → back to the question (Felicia, p. 483):** "I would read the whole question and then explore the whole graphic [looking for y and w]. Find our letters. Now let's find all the numbers. Then look at the angles … Then once we explored the graphic, go back to the question and read it." — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1192227.pdf)
- **Modeling with a vocabulary check (Felicia, p. 483):** "'Janet drew this isosceles trapezoid.' [I'll ask,] 'What does trapezoid mean?' Then I model what am I looking for in this problem." - **"What we know / what we have to find" (Elle, p. 483):** "[Students] get lost in the clutter … We go back to the question and just write out what we know and what we have to find out." Carol (p. 481): "for a blind student, I often have them write it down. So now they've taken this five lines of text information, and now they have their two things they are looking for or three things." — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1192227.pdf)
- **Same terminology as the class teacher (Elle, p. 483):** "I use the same language [consistently] with my students and then I try to use the language of their general ed[ucation] teachers. So if they are saying x-y graph, or Cartesian, coordinate graph, whatever it is, that's the language that they hear from a few sources." — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1192227.pdf)
- **Layered guidance (Dawn, p. 483):** "I have some students that need a lot of information, a lot of guidance, and some of them kind of get almost bogged down by too much information. I present [instruction] in a consistent manner, but kind of layer it where they need more assistance." — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1192227.pdf)
- **Simplify, then hand back (Elle, p. 480):** "I start by eliminating some of the clutter and then bringing it back and showing them how they can do that themselves." — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1192227.pdf)
- **Feedback timing (Elle, pp. 483–484):** "a lot of them are waiting for that prompt if they are right or wrong. They don't want to move on until they've heard that they are right." — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1192227.pdf)
- **Content before the graphic (p. 481).** Teachers stressed "having the student spend time understanding the content before going to the graphic … and of supporting the student in identifying in the content what is being asked for." - **Multiple media (Kate, p. 484):** "I have a student who needed all three media to be able to understand and gather information from different graphics." The teachers "noted the importance of having an auditory description of the graphic that accompanies the print or braille hard copy graphic." — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1192227.pdf)

**Gulley et al. (2017), reader-scribe dialogue — [TAM METİN]** (pp. 466–468)
- **Overview in vocabulary instead of symbols.** For a rational-expression division the student first hears "rational divided by rational." Asking for a numerator yields "term + term – constant" instead of "x² + 2x − 8." Terms are defined as "elements that are being added or subtracted." — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1156239.pdf)
- **Verbatim exchange (p. 467):** Student: "Cancel them. Are there any other matching factors found in both the numerator and the denominator?" Reader-scribe: "Yes, I see a factor of the quantity x + 4 in the numerator and the denominator." … Student: "Read what is there." Reader-scribe: "A rational with a numerator of 4x and a denominator of 2." — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1156239.pdf)
- **Ambiguity warning (p. 468).** "A reader-scribe who mistakenly says 'the square root of x + 1 divided by 2' will put the student at a disadvantage because that statement can be interpreted five different ways." A layered rendering of the quadratic formula is given: "x equals a rational" → "The numerator of the rational is term plus or minus term" → "The first term is negative b, and the second term is a square root" → "The inside of the square root is term minus term" → … → "The denominator of the rational is the factor 2a." — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1156239.pdf)
- **Training and limitations (pp. 468–469).** "The role of the reader-scribe must be filled by an individual who has mastery over the course content, and training is critically important." Limitations: "subjectivity … between the student and the reader-scribe" and a "highly labor-intensive process." Proposed remedy: "Software would eliminate the person who is in the role of the reader-scribe and improve the autonomy of students." — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1156239.pdf)

**ETS Guidelines for Test Readers (the neutral, no-hint register) — [TAM METİN]**
- **Neutrality:** "Your task is to read only the test questions. Don't try to solve problems or determine the correct answer as you read because this may result in an unconscious pause or change in inflection that could be misleading." — [ETS](https://www.ets.org/disabilities/resources/test-reader-guidelines.html)
- **No emphasis or interpretation:**
  - "Give special emphasis to words printed in boldface, italics or capitals and tell the test taker that the words are printed that way. Don't give your own emphasis to words not emphasized in print."
  - "Avoid getting into conversation about the test questions, but try to respond to the test taker's questions by repeating the item, words or instructions as needed." — [ETS](https://www.ets.org/disabilities/resources/test-reader-guidelines.html)
- **Offering re-reads:**
  - On a long pause: "Do you want me to read the question again … or any part of it?"
  - "In rereading questions, be careful to avoid any special emphasis."
  - "Read each … answer choice with equal stress"; if the student answers early, "ask if you should read the other response options." — [ETS](https://www.ets.org/disabilities/resources/test-reader-guidelines.html)
- **Reading math:**
  - "Mathematical expressions must be read precisely and with care … for a test taker who has no visual reference … it may be preferable for the reader to silently read the entire question before reading it aloud."
  - "Use technically correct yet simple terms and be consistent in the treatment of similar expressions."
- **Rules for reading math aloud:**
  - Juxtaposed letters: "xy" is read "x y," or "x times y" if unclear.
  - Say "capital R."
  - Simple fractions: "five sixths."
  - Complex fractions: "a fraction with numerator b plus d and denominator c"; "If there is any question as to where the fraction ends, say 'end fraction.'"
  - Negative numbers: "negative five," not "minus five."
  - Parentheses: "quantity … close quantity" or "paren … close paren"; with "quantity" in complex expressions, "announce where enclosed portions end by saying 'end quantity.'"
  - "Use pauses to audibly group sections of an expression together."
  - Equations: read the number, then "Give the meaning of each letter or symbol," then read the equation.
  - Scribed arithmetic: the student may dictate steps ("Seven times one is seven. Put down the seven…"). — [ETS](https://www.ets.org/disabilities/resources/test-reader-guidelines.html)

**Description quantity (Wall Emerson & Anderson, 2018) — [TAM METİN]**
- Students' perceptions: "Too much and not enough information were both cited as bad aspects of the descriptions." Asked whether they wanted "absolutely everything on a page described," 24 said yes, 13 no and 7 "maybe/it depends." Several noted that "only audio was provided … and a braille version, a tactile graphic, or both were not also provided." — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1175990.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Two registers for the assistant**, each grounded in a source:

  | Mode | Source model | What it does |
  |---|---|---|
  | **(A) Reader** (default: "read and explain without hints") | ETS test-reader rules | Precise, neutral, repeatable reading. No added emphasis, no strategy talk. Answer only questions about what the problem says: repeat, spell, or read a part. |
  | **(B) Teacher** (only when the student asks for a solution) | TVI behaviors in Rosenblum 2018 | Model first, then fade. Use "what do we know / what must we find." Use classroom vocabulary. Layer the amount of detail. |

- **"Explain without hints" fits a structure-only overview.** A Process-Driven-Math-style overview ("Bir kesir bölü bir kesir" / "a fraction divided by a fraction"; "terim artı terim eksi sabit" / "term plus term minus constant") explains the problem's *structure* without solving it. So does an NCAM-style element inventory of a figure: type, title, axes, labels, values. One caution: the Process-Driven Math overview vocabulary partly reveals the method (e.g., "the numerator is a quadratic"). The assistant should name only surface categories (kesir, terim, sabit, kök — fraction, term, constant, root) and never procedural ones ("çarpanlarına ayrılabilir" — "can be factored") in no-hint mode.
- **Restating "what is given / what is asked" is the line between explaining and hinting.** It is a TVI staple (Rosenblum 2018: "write out what we know and what we have to find out") and is arguably not a hint. It can be offered in no-hint mode, but only as a restatement of the problem's own content, never as a choice of method.
- **TVIs rarely ask questions (Rosenblum 2020).** The assistant's comprehension checks should be confirmatory and few ("Soruyu tekrar okumamı ister misin?" — "Do you want me to read the question again?"), not Socratic, while in no-hint mode.
- **Answer checking should give clear, timely right/wrong feedback.** Students wait for confirmation before moving on (Rosenblum 2018), and audio right/wrong feedback was a reason students preferred the AnimalWatch app (Beal & Rosenblum 2018). In no-hint mode, the feedback should state correct or incorrect, and possibly *where* the student's answer differs (e.g., the sign). It should not say *how* to fix it. This boundary is a design inference.

### Gaps
- **Real classroom TVI-student math dialogue.** No transcribed corpus was found. Rosenblum 2020 used hypothetical students and recorded monologues. Rosenblum 2018 is teachers' self-reports. Gulley 2017 shows constructed example dialogues.
- **Algebra verbalization.** No study codes TVIs' verbalization of *symbolic* algebra (as opposed to graphics) or links verbal behaviors to student outcomes. The authors of Rosenblum 2020 note that "the actual effectiveness of the teachers' descriptions with students was not assessed."
- **Turkish TVI practice.** No source on how Turkish TVIs or math teachers read math aloud was examined.

## 3. What "step-by-step presentation" means in this literature, and how an audio-only assistant can replicate it

### Takeaway
The phrase covers four operationally distinct techniques:
1. **Cumulative frame-by-frame build-up** of a graphic, each frame paired with a matching narration segment (Tactile Data Comics).
2. **Layered revelation of an expression**: a structural overview in vocabulary first, then sub-parts on request (Process-Driven Math; ETS "Describe" mode and zoom).
3. **Chunked navigation at structural boundaries**, ideally segmented automatically rather than by the student (ETS arrow keys and zoom; Riga's recommendation).
4. **A fixed exploration script the student internalizes** (title → type → axes → scale → key → question) from TVI practice.

An audio-only assistant can implement 2, 3 and 4 fully, and 1 only as narration. The tactile half of 1, which produced the measured 76.6% vs 70.1% gain, cannot be replicated by audio, and that gain was not significant specifically for math.

### Cited Findings
- **Tactile Data Comics (definition) [TAM METİN].** "Tactile data comics use a step-by-step approach that breaks down complex images into smaller, digestible components. In this method, each component builds upon the previous one, retaining earlier content while introducing new elements … each component is accompanied by verbal description of the presented content." — [SMU repository](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/sis_research/10704/)
  - **Two defining features:** "a discrete frame-by-frame presentation of progressively evolving content, just like comics," chosen over continuous animation "to reduce learning difficulties," and "pairing of each component with a verbal description." The graphic is segmented "based on the logical structure of the content," and the narration is edited "for coherent and logical transition … so that they can form a complete and coherent verbal story."
  - **The problem it addresses:** "regardless of which step of the content the teacher is introducing, the static tactile images from the textbooks remain unchanged and do not adapt to the teacher's narration." Participants "were notified whenever the graphic changed." — [SMU repository](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/sis_research/10704/)
- **Tactile Data Comics (limits of verbal-only presentation) [TAM METİN].** P13: "It's very difficult to construct the corresponding graphic in my mind with purely verbal narration. Even if the verbal narration is clear, there can still be deviations when constructing the image, and once there's a deviation, it becomes hard to understand the subsequent descriptions." The approach also has a cost: some participants with less tactile experience relied more on narration, and one reported "every time the device refreshes a new image, I have to reconstruct the framework of the graphic in my mind." — [SMU repository](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/sis_research/10704/)
- **Important for interpreting the gain [TAM METİN].** The verbal-only condition already used the same segmented narration with 5-second pauses at section breaks. The +6.5-point gain is therefore attributable to adding the *synchronized cumulative tactile frames*, not to stepwise narration as such.
  - **DÜZELTME.** The earlier notes inferred that "stepwise sequencing itself adds about 6.5 percentage points." That reading is not supported. — [SMU repository](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/sis_research/10704/)
- **Process-Driven Math (layering) [TAM METİN].** "Numbers and symbols are temporarily hidden behind layers of appropriate mathematic vocabulary." The student "can often make decisions that advance the simplification process without having to hear the actual numbers and symbols in a particular part of the expression." After each transformation, the reader-scribe restates the new top-level structure ("rational times rational") and records every step "so that every decision made is available to the student for review" (pp. 465–467). — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1156239.pdf)
- **ETS RR-17-13 (navigation as step-by-step) [TAM METİN].**
  - "move forward or backward through any expression in 'chunks,' the size and organization of which are controlled by the zoom in/out and navigation mode settings."
  - Describe mode: "the expression 3/4 + (x+1)/(x−1) would speak as 'fraction plus fraction.'"
  - "Where Am I … indicates the larger context and location of the current chunk."
  - Three chunking modes: Character, Simple, and Enhanced ("making the greatest use of mathematical meaning").
  - Students rated arrow-key stepping and zoom highest. The overview ("Describe") and "Where Am I" were rarely found useful, at 12 and 9 of 20 "no easier."
  - Each of the three modes was someone's favorite. — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1168599.pdf)
- **Riga & Kouroupetroglou (segmentation level) [TAM METİN].** Automatic segmentation "could be performed on different structural levels and not random places, thereby allowing for users to listen to complete sub-expressions." In textbooks a long expression "is usually 'built' in/from several steps/expressions," but whether this prior knowledge helps recall "is unproven." — [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/9e8d/57260f7579c4e7c015533d31336d6ef3b60d.pdf)
- **TVI exploration script [TAM METİN].** Felicia's "very specific step-by-step process" (title → x-axis label → y-axis → scale → gridlines) and the general checklist (title, graphic type, key, what to look for) (Rosenblum et al. 2018, pp. 482–483). — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1192227.pdf)
- **Klingenberg digital review [TAM METİN].** Microworld programs "can be configured to suite a wide range of learning modes, from guiding the student step-by-step and to more open-ended explorations," and "'guided tasks' are most often used by the youngest students." — [NTNU Open](https://hdl.handle.net/11250/2626773)
- **AnimalWatch-VI (step-by-step help sequencing) [TAM METİN].** "If a wrong answer was entered, hint 1 became available, followed by hint 2 … The first hint guided the student to select the type of computation needed, and the second hint guided the student in setting up the problem." Hints had to be actively opened. "After three incorrect answers, they could access a brief narrated video that outlined the solution; the solution was also available after a correct answer was entered. Students always had the option to give up on (skip) a problem and view the solution." — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1172160.pdf)

### Inferences
**Audio-only replication protocol for the assistant.** This is a design inference built from the sources above.

1. **Silent full parse first.** Following ETS reader rules, OCR the photo into a structured tree (MathML/LaTeX), confirm it, and never read raw OCR. Optionally confirm low-confidence symbols with the student ("Burada 'x kare' mi yazıyor, 'x çarpı 2' mi?" — "Does it say 'x squared' or 'x times 2' here?").
2. **Overview layer.** This mirrors Process-Driven Math and ETS Describe mode.
   - State the problem type and its top-level structure in surface vocabulary, e.g., "Bir denklem: sol tarafta bir kesir, sağ tarafta iki terim var." ("An equation: there is a fraction on the left and two terms on the right.")
   - ETS students rarely found Describe useful, so keep the overview to one sentence and move straight into navigable reading.
3. **Complexity gate.** Following Riga, count C = S + N + O.
   - **C ≤ 10:** read the whole expression once, with lexical boundary words.
   - **C ≥ 11:** read by structural chunks (numerator, then denominator; each term; inside each root) and offer to go "deeper" or "back up." This is the audio analogue of ETS zoom.
4. **Cumulative restatement.** This is the audio analogue of the Tactile Data Comics frame and the Process-Driven Math record. After each chunk or each solution step, restate the *current whole* briefly before adding the next piece ("Şu ana kadar: pay 4x artı 8. Şimdi payda…" — "So far: the numerator is 4x plus 8. Now the denominator…"). This keeps earlier content "retained," as the comics' frames do, without relying on working memory.
5. **Student-controlled stepping.** Provide voice commands equivalent to ETS arrow keys, zoom and "Where Am I": "devam" (continue), "geri" (back), "payı oku" (read the numerator), "neredeyim" (where am I), "baştan oku" (read from the start), "yavaş oku" (read slowly). Keep "where am I" short, since ETS students rated it low.
6. **Figures.** Use the TVI script order (type → title → axes/labels → scale → key → data) and NCAM-level detail. Wall Emerson & Anderson found NCAM-level detail roughly doubled correct answers over terse descriptions (28.8% vs 13.6%). Tell the student honestly when a figure type (pie chart, map, complex geometry) is poorly served by audio, and suggest tactile or graph-paper support. Equations embedded as images got only 5.7% correct in that study.
7. **Solve-on-request.** Follow the AnimalWatch sequence and the TVI "model then fade" approach.
   - **When the student asks for a solution:** go step by step, state the reason for each step, and record each step so it can be replayed (Process-Driven Math).
   - **Before giving a full solution:** offer, but do not force, one intermediate level ("Sadece ilk adımı mı söyleyeyim, yoksa tüm çözümü mü?" — "Shall I tell you just the first step, or the whole solution?"). This respects the no-unsolicited-hints rule.

### Gaps
- **Audio-only step-by-step vs whole-expression delivery has not been tested.** No study compares stepwise, cumulative-restatement narration with whole-expression narration for symbolic algebra with blind secondary students. The Tactile Data Comics verbal arm was already segmented, and Riga used no navigation.
- **Hint-free tutoring is unstudied.** No study tests tutoring for VI students that explains without hints (no-hint explanation plus answer checking). AnimalWatch-VI always offered hints after errors.
- **Optimal speech rate and pause length for spoken math** was not reported in any full text read. ETS mentions only that students found it "hard to slow down," and Riga used the default rate.
